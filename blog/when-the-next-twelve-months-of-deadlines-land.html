Calendar

When the next twelve months of deadlines land

The filing year is not evenly distributed. Four months carry most of the weight, and the last week of January carries more than the other eleven months combined.

10 November 20266 min read

Ask a business owner when their filings are due and you will usually get two answers: April 15, and "the quarterly thing." Both are true, and together they miss most of the year. The calendar has a shape — a few dense clusters separated by long quiet stretches — and knowing the shape is what makes the list manageable.

Here is a walk through a typical twelve months for a California business with employees and a seller's permit. The exact set changes with entity type, but the clusters do not.

January: the pile-up

January carries two different kinds of work at once. The first is the fourth-quarter estimated tax payment, due January 15 for individuals and pass-through owners. The second is the annual payroll paperwork, all of it due at the end of the month: Form 941 for the fourth quarter, Form 940 for federal unemployment, W-2s to employees and the Social Security Administration, and 1099-NEC for contractors paid $600 or more.

In California, add the DE 9 and DE 9C — nominally due January 1, with the delinquency date at the end of January — and the fourth-quarter sales tax return on January 31. That is five federal filings and two state filings inside roughly two weeks, and it is the single densest stretch of the year.

March: the pass-through month

March 15 is the information-return deadline for partnerships, multi-member LLCs, and S corporations. Form 1065 or Form 1120-S is due, along with K-1s for the owners. The entity may owe no tax at all; the return exists so the owners can file theirs.

This is the date that surprises sole proprietors who later become an LLC with a partner. The business did not necessarily get more complicated, but the filing did: a separate return, a month earlier, with schedules that flow to other people's taxes.

April: two deadlines, not one

April 15 is the one everyone knows: Form 1040 for sole proprietors and single-member LLCs, Form 1120 for C corporations, and the first quarterly estimate for the year. In California it is also the $800 minimum annual tax for LLCs, corporations, and partnerships.

April 30 is the one people forget. The first-quarter Form 941 is due, the first-quarter sales tax return is due, and the DE 9 and DE 9C reach their delinquency date. April is a two-deadline month, and the second one is a payroll deadline rather than an income tax deadline, which is why it hides in a different mental bucket.

June and September: the quiet ones

June 15 is the second estimated tax payment. September 15 is the third, and it is also the extended deadline for partnership and S corporation returns. Neither month has the volume of January or April, which makes them easy to underrate: an estimate missed in June is still a penalty, even if no form is due.

October: extensions come due

October 15 is the extended deadline for personal returns and C corporation returns. October 31 closes the third quarter: Form 941, the California payroll report, and the sales tax return. If April was the month of original filings, October is the month of the ones that were put off.

December: the estimate nobody expects

C corporations have a fourth estimated tax payment on December 15, not in January. Owners who learned the individual schedule — April, June, September, January — often miss it. It is the clearest example of why the entity type matters: the same four payments land on different dates depending on the return behind them.

One date that breaks the pattern

The California Statement of Information does not follow the tax year at all. It follows the anniversary of registration. An LLC registered in May files every two years by the end of May, in years matching the registration year's parity. A corporation files annually in its registration month. This is the one item on the calendar that can land in any month, which is exactly why it gets forgotten.

The two weeks that decide the month

Every cluster above is a filing date, not a work date. The form has to exist before it can be filed, and its inputs arrive late: a K-1 depends on the entity return closing, a payroll return depends on the quarter's deposits reconciling, a sales tax return depends on three months of register totals. Working backward two weeks from each cluster is the difference between filing and filing late.

That makes the real calendar slightly longer than the list of due dates. January's work starts in the second week of the month, which collides with the holidays. March's starts in late February. April's starts at the end of March — the same week the first-quarter payroll deposits come due.

What changes with the entity

A sole proprietor with no employees sees four dates: three estimates and the personal return. Add a single-member LLC and the California $800 tax and Statement of Information appear. Add employees and the quarterly payroll cycle begins. Add a partner and the March information return appears. Switch to an S corporation and the March date stays but the form changes, while payroll for the owner becomes mandatory in practice.

The clusters are offsets from the quarter or the year end, which is why they repeat. Once you know the offset, you can regenerate the calendar for any year without memorizing it.
