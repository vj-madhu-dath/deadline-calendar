Comparison

The best free compliance calendars, compared

Five ways to keep track of filing dates without paying for software. Each one is genuinely useful, and each one stops somewhere specific. The point of the comparison is to find that edge before a deadline does.

18 November 20266 min read

"Free" covers a wide range of arrangements. An IRS page is free and authoritative but does not know what kind of business you run. A spreadsheet is free and endlessly flexible but only as accurate as the list you typed into it. A calendar app is free and already on your phone but contains no rules at all. Comparing them on price alone misses the thing that decides whether a filing gets missed.

So the comparison here uses four tests: does it know your entity, does it show the source, can someone else read it, and who keeps it current. Every option below fails at least one.

1. The calendar builder on this site

Six fields — entity type, payroll headcount, contractors, state, seller's permit, and registration month — produce a twelve-month list of federal and California dates. Each item carries the form name, the agency, a sentence explaining why it applies, and a link to the primary source. The list exports as an .ics file or prints as a checklist.

Where it stops: California is the only state, fiscal years are not supported, and a date that lands on a weekend is flagged rather than moved. A weekday federal holiday is not detected at all. It is also a static page maintained by hand, which means a rule can lag a real-world change until someone files a correction.

2. The IRS tax calendar

The IRS publishes a calendar of federal due dates, and Publication 509 collects them in one document. It is free, it comes from the agency that decides the dates, and it is updated when the forms are.

Where it stops: it is federal. It does not know you are an LLC, so it cannot tell you that your return is a 1065 rather than a 1040. It does not include the California $800 tax or the Statement of Information, and it does not know whether you hold a seller's permit. It is the best possible source for a question about one federal form and a poor substitute for a business calendar.

3. A spreadsheet you build yourself

A single sheet with columns for date, form, agency, owner, and status is a legitimate compliance system. It is free, it is portable, and it forces you to read the rules once, which is more than most tools do.

Where it stops: it is a snapshot. Nothing in the sheet knows that adding a partner turns April into March, or that registering in a new state adds a whole column of dates. It also has no reminders unless you connect it to something, and the person who built it is usually the only one who understands its conventions.

4. A shared calendar with recurring events

Google Calendar, Apple Calendar, and Outlook all let you create repeating events with alerts, and all three are free. As an artifact this is strong: the dates live where people already look, and a second person can subscribe to the same calendar.

Where it stops: at the rule set. A recurring event means you already know the date and the rule. It cannot generate the list, cannot explain why a date applies, and will happily repeat a deadline that no longer exists. Used as the destination for an exported list, it is excellent. Used as the source, it is a memory aid with no memory.

5. A template for Notion, Sheets, or a docs app

Pre-built compliance templates circulate widely, often free, often with a checklist grouped by quarter. They are a reasonable starting point for someone who has never written a filing list and needs to see the shape of one.

Where it stops: a template is someone else's business. It may assume a Delaware corporation, a fiscal year, or a state you do not operate in. Product plans and pricing change too, so a template that is free today may sit behind a paid tier later. Read the content, keep the structure, and replace every rule with one you have verified.

How to choose

Start from the number of dates. A sole proprietor with no employees and no state registration has three or four a year, all federal. The IRS calendar plus a phone alert is enough; there is no reason to build a system for four dates.

Add a California LLC registration and the count roughly doubles, with a biennial form that follows an anniversary rather than the tax year. That is the point where a list generated from facts beats a list typed by hand, because the anniversary is exactly the item people forget.

Add employees and the quarterly payroll cycle makes the count structural: four 941s, four state payroll reports, four sales tax returns if there is a permit, plus January's annual forms. At that volume the question stops being "which tool" and becomes "who owns each date," and any of these options works once an owner is named.

The combination that holds up

Use one source to generate the list, one calendar to hold it, and one recurring review to keep it honest. In practice: generate the dates from a tool that knows your entity, export them into the calendar the business already checks, and put a fifteen-minute quarterly review on the calendar itself.

That last item is the one no free tool supplies. Every option above can hold a date; none of them will notice that you hired someone in March, registered in a second state, or changed your year end. A calendar without a review is a snapshot, and snapshots go stale quietly.
