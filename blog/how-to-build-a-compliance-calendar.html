How to build a compliance calendar in one afternoon

You do not need a new tool to do this. You need one list of facts, one list of sources, and one place where the two meet. Here is the method, with or without software.

14 November 20266 min read

Most compliance calendars fail for the same reason: they are built from forms instead of facts. Someone collects a list of deadlines from an article, puts them in a spreadsheet, and six months later the business has changed — a partner joined, payroll started, a permit was added — and the spreadsheet has not.

Build from the facts that generate the dates, and the calendar can be rebuilt in an hour whenever those facts change. Five steps, roughly one afternoon.

Step 1: inventory the business

Write down four facts and two registrations. The facts: entity type, whether you have W-2 employees, whether you pay contractors $600 or more, and your fiscal year end. The registrations: any state tax account, and any seller's permit or sales tax account.

Then add the two details that do not fit a checkbox: the month and year the entity was registered with the state, and any city business license with its own renewal date. Registration month matters because entity filings follow the anniversary, not the tax year. City licenses are local and easy to forget precisely because they are not in the state or federal list.

If two owners disagree about the entity type, stop here and resolve it. A calendar built on the wrong structure is worse than no calendar.

Step 2: collect the primary sources

For each area, save a link to the agency page rather than a date. The IRS form page for payroll and income tax. The California EDD payroll calendar. The CDTFA filing dates page for sales tax. The FTB business due dates page for the $800 tax and state returns. The Secretary of State page for the Statement of Information. OSHA for the 300A posting window.

The links matter more than the dates because the dates change and the links do not. When a threshold moves or a weekend shifts a deadline, the page reflects it and a copied table does not.

One habit makes the list worth keeping: check each page's own revision date before copying anything from it. Agency pages usually say when they were last reviewed. A page untouched for three years is either stable or abandoned, and only one of those is safe to trust.

Store the links somewhere durable rather than in a bookmark bar. A shared note, a folder in the business drive, or a section at the bottom of the calendar file all work. The test is whether the next person to hold the calendar can find the source without asking you.

Step 3: turn rules into dates

Now apply the facts to the sources. Entity type selects the income tax form and its date. Employees add the quarterly payroll cycle and the January annual forms. A seller's permit adds the sales tax return and its frequency. A California registration adds the $800 tax, the Statement of Information, and the state payroll returns.

Write each result as a full sentence, not a fragment: "Form 941 for the fourth quarter, due January 31, filed by the bookkeeper." A calendar entry that says only "941" will be unreadable in eleven months, when the person who created it may not be the person reading it.

Include the extension dates for the returns you are likely to extend. An extension is a date on the calendar too, and it is the one people forget to note because they think of it as permission rather than an obligation.

Step 4: assign an owner and a reminder

Every date needs one name next to it. Not a team, not "the office" — one person who is expected to notice. Shared ownership is how a filing gets missed while everyone assumes it was handled.

Then put two reminders on each date: one thirty days out for preparation, and one seven days out for the filing itself. A single reminder on the due date is a notification, not a plan; if the return needs a K-1 or a payroll reconciliation, thirty days is the minimum useful warning.

Export the result to the calendar the business already uses. A calendar that lives in a second app nobody opens is a spreadsheet with better typography.

Step 5: review quarterly, refresh annually

Once a quarter, ask four questions: did we hire anyone, register anything new, change an address, or receive an agency notice? Each yes can add or move a date. Fifteen minutes is enough.

Once a year, before January, re-check the rule set against the sources. Thresholds move, forms get replaced, and relief provisions expire. The annual refresh is also the moment to confirm the entity type and the registration month, since both drive dates that never appear on a federal calendar.

The one-page checklist

  • Entity type, employees, contractors, fiscal year end written down
  • State tax account and seller's permit confirmed
  • Registration month and year recorded
  • Six primary source links saved
  • Each date written as a sentence with a form name and agency
  • One owner per date
  • Thirty-day and seven-day reminders set
  • Exported to the calendar the business actually uses
  • Quarterly review scheduled, annual refresh scheduled

The mistakes that undo it

Three failures account for most missed filings. The first is a federal-only calendar, which omits the California annual tax and the Statement of Information. The second is a calendar with no owner, where every date is everyone's responsibility. The third is a calendar that is never refreshed, so it confidently lists a form that no longer exists or a date that moved.

None of these are solved by a better reminder. They are solved by building from facts, keeping the sources, and reviewing on a schedule. That is an afternoon's work, and it is the difference between a list you trust and a list you hope is still right.
