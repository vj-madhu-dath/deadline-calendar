Foundations

How business filing deadlines are actually scheduled

Open a tax calendar and the dates look arbitrary: a 15th here, a last day there, a January 31 that seems to belong to the previous year. They are not arbitrary. Almost every date is an offset from one of three clocks.

2 November 20266 min read

Small business owners usually meet the filing calendar one form at a time. The accountant mentions Form 941, so a reminder goes in for April. Later someone asks about the Statement of Information, and a second reminder goes in for a month that turns out to be the registration anniversary. The result is a pile of dates with no shape, and every new form feels like a surprise.

There is a shape. Federal and California deadlines are generated by three clocks that run at different speeds, and each clock has its own arithmetic. Once you know which clock a form belongs to, you can predict the date instead of hunting for it.

The payroll clock runs quarterly

Payroll is the fastest clock. Once you pay a W-2 employee, Form 941 is due four times a year: April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31. Each date is the last day of the month after a calendar quarter, which gives you thirty days to reconcile wages and deposits.

California's DE 9 and DE 9C follow the same quarterly shape but a different convention. The nominal due date is the first day of the month — January 1, April 1, July 1, October 1 — with a one-month delinquency window before penalties begin. Two agencies, same quarter, two different dates. A list that says "quarterly payroll" without naming the form and the agency is not much use.

January also carries the annual payroll paperwork that people forget: W-2s to employees and the Social Security Administration, Form 940 for federal unemployment, and 1099-NEC for contractors paid $600 or more. Those are annual forms, but they land in the same week as the fourth-quarter 941, which is why the last week of January feels heavier than the rest of the year.

The income tax clock follows the entity

The second clock is annual, and its date is set by what the business is. A sole proprietor and a single-member LLC file with the owner's Form 1040 on April 15. A partnership or multi-member LLC files Form 1065 on March 15, because the owners need K-1s before their own returns are due. An S corporation files Form 1120-S on that same March 15 for the same reason. A C corporation files Form 1120 on April 15. A nonprofit files the 990 series on May 15.

The 15th is not a coincidence. It is the 15th day of the third month after a December year end for pass-throughs, and the 15th day of the fourth month for everyone else — roughly one extra month for the entities whose owners need the information before they can file.

Extensions stretch the clock but do not stop it. Form 7004 gives partnerships and S corporations until September 15. A C corporation gets until October 15, and a personal return gets until October 15 as well. An extension moves the filing date, not the payment date. The tax is still due in April.

The state clock is layered on top

California adds a third layer, and it is the one most likely to be missing from a federal-only calendar. The $800 minimum annual tax for LLCs, corporations, and partnerships is due April 15 for a calendar year. The Statement of Information runs on the anniversary of registration, not the tax year: LLCs file every two years in the month they registered, in years matching the registration year's parity, while corporations file annually in that month. Sales tax returns are quarterly for most accounts, due the last day of the month after the quarter. Pay data reporting lands in May for employers with 100 or more employees.

Notice that these are three different kinds of dates: a fixed calendar date, an anniversary, and a reporting window. A generic monthly checklist cannot represent all three. A calendar can, because a calendar is just dates with labels attached.

Why the dates cluster

Put the clocks together and the clusters appear. January 31 is the payroll annualization plus the fourth-quarter return. March 15 is the pass-through deadline. April 15 is the individual and corporate deadline plus California's minimum tax. April 30 is the first-quarter payroll return plus the first-quarter sales tax return. September 15 and October 15 are extensions. December 15 is the fourth-quarter corporate estimate, which surprises people who expect January.

The gap between March and April exists for a reason. Pass-through owners cannot finish their personal returns until they have a K-1, so the entity files first and the owner files a month later. That sequencing is why a partnership return is not optional paperwork even when the business itself owes no tax.

None of this requires memorizing twenty-four rules. It requires knowing which clock you are on and doing the arithmetic once. The arithmetic is stable from year to year; only the weekend and holiday adjustments move.

Put the clocks on one page

The practical move is to write down four facts about the business: entity type, whether you run payroll, whether you hold a seller's permit, and the month and year you registered in California. Those four facts select almost every date that applies to you.

Then put the dates in one calendar that someone else can read, rather than four reminders in four apps. That is the whole method. The list still needs an annual review, because rates and thresholds change, and a fiscal year shifts everything. But the shape of the year does not change, and once you can see the shape, a missed date is a choice rather than an accident.
