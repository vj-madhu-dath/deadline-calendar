Sources

Where to find your official filing dates

The fastest way to get a date wrong is to trust a list that does not say where it came from. Here is which agency owns which part of the year, and how to read the pages they publish.

8 November 20266 min read

Search for "small business tax deadlines" and you will find dozens of lists that agree with each other and disagree with the agencies. The disagreement is usually not malicious. It comes from copying last year's table, leaving out the state layer, or confusing the last day to file with the last day before a penalty starts.

The fix is to go one level up: find the agency that actually administers the form, and read its page. There are six you need for a federal and California business.

If a date is not on one of these sites, it is either a local rule, a court deadline, or somebody's summary. That does not make it wrong, but it does mean you should find the underlying source before relying on it.

The federal layer

The IRS publishes a page for each form with its due date, instructions, and current revision. For a single form, that page is the best source because it is updated with the form. For the year as a whole, Publication 509, Tax Calendars, collects the federal dates in one place.

What the federal pages do not know is your entity. The 1040, 1065, 1120-S, 1120, and 990 pages all exist, but nothing on irs.gov tells you which one is yours. That mapping — sole proprietor to 1040, partnership to 1065, and so on — is the part a general calendar leaves to the reader.

California payroll

The EDD publishes a payroll tax calendar that lists the DE 9 and DE 9C every quarter. Read it carefully, because it uses two dates. The nominal due date is the first day of the month following the quarter — January 1, April 1, July 1, October 1. The "delinquent if not filed by" date is the end of that month.

The gap is a grace period, not an extension. Filing between the first and the end of the month is treated as timely, but the obligation began on the first. A calendar that lists only the delinquent date is technically useful and practically misleading: it trains you to file at the last possible moment.

Sales tax

The CDTFA assigns each account a filing frequency when it registers — quarterly, quarterly prepay, monthly, yearly, or fiscal yearly. The due date follows from the frequency, not from a single statewide table. Most small accounts are quarterly, with the return due the last day of the month after the quarter: April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31.

This is why a generic "sales tax is due quarterly" line is not enough. A monthly filer has twelve returns a year, and a prepay account has additional dates inside each quarter. The agency's filing-dates page lays out each schedule separately.

Income and franchise tax

The FTB's business due dates page covers the California returns: Form 100 for corporations, 100S for S corporations, 565 for partnerships, 568 for LLCs, and 199 for exempt organizations. For a calendar-year business, the recurring date is April 15 — the 15th day of the fourth month — and the $800 minimum tax is due with it for entities that owe it.

Extensions are automatic but do not extend the payment. That distinction appears on the FTB page and is easy to miss in a summary that only lists the extended filing date.

Entity filings

The Secretary of State handles the filings that have nothing to do with tax: the Statement of Information, which keeps the entity's officers, addresses, and agent current. LLCs file every two years in the month they registered, in years matching the registration year's parity. Stock corporations file annually in the registration month.

The filing window is wider than the deadline suggests. It opens six months before the registration month and closes at the end of it. That is useful if you like to file early, and dangerous if you mistake the opening of the window for the due date.

Why secondary sources drift

Four things go wrong in copied lists. Weekend and holiday adjustments are applied inconsistently, so a date that moved to Monday still appears as Sunday. "Due" and "delinquent" are treated as synonyms when an agency publishes both. Annual updates lag, so last year's threshold appears next to this year's form. And fiscal-year dates are collapsed into calendar-year dates because most readers are assumed to be on a December year.

None of these are exotic failures. They are the normal cost of summarizing a primary source, which is why the primary source is worth keeping a link to.

How to read an official page

Ask four questions before you copy a date. Is this the filing date or the payment date? Is this the due date or the delinquency date? Does an extension change both, or only the filing? And does the date depend on my entity type or filing frequency? A page that answers all four is a page you can trust; a page that answers none is a page to bookmark and verify later.

If a page answers all four but was last revised three years ago, treat the date as a hypothesis and confirm it against the form's current instructions.

Keep the links, not just the dates. A date is a claim, and a claim without a source cannot be checked when it matters.
