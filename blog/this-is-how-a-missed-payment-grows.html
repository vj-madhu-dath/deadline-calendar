California

This is how a missed $800 payment grows

Eight hundred dollars is not a crisis. The penalty and interest around it are capped and slow. What is not slow, and not capped, is losing the entity's standing over a form that has nothing to do with the tax.

18 November 20266 min read

California's minimum annual tax applies to LLCs, corporations, and partnerships registered in the state, and it is owed for the privilege of existing rather than for making money. An LLC with no revenue in its first year still owes it. That surprises people, and the surprise is where the trouble starts: a bill arrives for a business that has not opened yet, gets set aside, and then a second notice arrives a year later with more digits on it.

It is worth knowing what those extra digits are made of, because they come from two separate systems that are easy to confuse.

The tax itself does not shrink

The $800 is a minimum, not a flat fee. Entities that owe more under the corporate franchise or income tax rules pay more, and the $800 is credited against that. For a small LLC or a corporation with modest California income, $800 is the whole bill.

It is also not prorated. Registering in October does not produce a quarter of the tax. And a business that never opened, never invoiced, and never had a customer still owes it for each year it remained registered. The only way to stop the annual charge is to dissolve or surrender the entity properly, which is its own filing.

The penalty formula

The Franchise Tax Board describes the late-payment penalty as 5 percent of the unpaid tax, plus 0.5 percent for each month or part of a month the tax remains unpaid, capped at 25 percent in total. Interest is added on top and is not part of that cap.

Read the formula closely and two things stand out. The 5 percent is charged once, immediately, so the first month is expensive relative to the ones that follow. The 0.5 percent is charged per month or part of a month, which means a payment that arrives two days after a month boundary is treated like a payment that arrived thirty days late. And the cap arrives at 25 percent, which for an $800 tax is $200 — reached after roughly forty months.

Interest is the part that keeps moving. The FTB sets its rate periodically, and it accrues on the unpaid tax and on the penalty. There is no 25 percent ceiling on it.

The pattern is worth noticing: after three years, the balance is about 1.4 times the original tax, and the penalty has not even reached its ceiling. That is real money, but it is not the catastrophic number a headline would suggest. The cap does its job. The reason this filing ruins small businesses is somewhere else.

The $250 that is not part of the tax

Separately from the tax, a California LLC must file a Statement of Information every two years, in its registration month, in years matching the registration year's parity. Corporations file one every year. The form updates the manager, the address, and the agent for service of process — the person a lawsuit is served on.

Miss it and the Secretary of State charges a $250 penalty. That penalty is per filing, not per month, and it is not reduced by paying the $800. It is a different agency, a different form, and a different account. A business can be perfectly current with the FTB and still be suspended by the Secretary of State for a form it never knew existed.

Suspension is the expensive part

An LLC whose powers are suspended cannot transact business in the ordinary way, and it loses access to the courts to enforce its contracts or collect a debt. A corporation in the same position is subject to forfeiture of its powers, rights, and privileges. In practice this surfaces at the worst possible moment: a lease renewal, a loan draw, a vendor onboarding, or a customer who runs a routine check and asks for a certificate of good standing that the state will not issue.

Clearing it usually means two steps in a specific order. Pay or file with the FTB to clear the tax side, then file the Statement of Information and pay the $250 to clear the Secretary of State side. A business that fixes one and not the other stays suspended, and the notices keep coming.

What actually resolves it

File the missing return, pay what is owed, and ask for relief in writing if the record supports it. The FTB can abate penalties for reasonable cause, and the Secretary of State will consider a written explanation, but neither happens automatically and neither removes interest.

Then decide whether the entity should still exist. An LLC that has been dormant for two years may be better dissolved than revived. The dissolution filing has its own final return and its own $800 for the year it operated, and it stops the clock in a way that ignoring the notices does not.

The mistake underneath

Almost every one of these cases starts with the same assumption: no income means no filing. For the $800, that is false. For the Statement of Information, income is irrelevant entirely. The tax is the price of registration, and the form is the price of staying in good standing.

Both dates are predictable. One is April 15. The other is the registration anniversary, which is why it belongs on a calendar with the month and year of formation written next to it.
