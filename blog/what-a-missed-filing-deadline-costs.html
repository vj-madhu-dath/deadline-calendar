Risk

What a missed filing deadline actually costs

People talk about "the penalty" as if it were one thing. A late filing can produce a penalty, a running interest charge, and a change in the business's legal standing — and the three behave differently.

4 November 20266 min read

The invoice for a missed deadline rarely arrives as a single line. It arrives as a notice with a penalty, a second notice with interest, and somewhere in the background a status change that nobody sees until a bank asks for a certificate of good standing. Treating those as one problem leads to the wrong fix: paying the balance while leaving the return unfiled, or filing the return while the clock on interest keeps running.

It helps to separate the three costs and understand what each one responds to.

The penalty is a percentage, not a bill

Federal penalties for late business returns are usually built as a monthly percentage of the unpaid tax. The failure-to-file penalty is generally 5 percent per month, capped at 25 percent. The failure-to-pay penalty is generally 0.5 percent per month, also capped at 25 percent. When both apply, the failure-to-file amount is reduced by the failure-to-pay amount for that month, so the two do not simply stack to 5.5 percent.

California uses a similar shape for its own returns: 5 percent plus 0.5 percent per month, capped at 25 percent, with interest on top. Payroll is harsher. The EDD describes a 15 percent penalty plus interest for late payroll tax payments, which is one reason the quarterly payroll clock deserves more attention than its routine suggests.

Two consequences follow. First, a return that shows no tax due has a much smaller penalty exposure than a return with a balance — but "smaller" is not zero, and the filing obligation still exists. Second, the penalty is calculated on the unpaid tax, not on the size of the business. A small company with a real balance can owe more in penalty than a larger company that filed on time and paid nothing.

The percentage is applied per return and per period, so a business that skips two years of the same form faces the calculation twice, not once.

Interest does not cap, and it compounds

Interest is the quieter charge. The IRS underpayment rate is the federal short-term rate plus three percentage points, compounded daily. California computes its own rate, which changes periodically. Unlike the penalty, interest is not capped at 25 percent, and it is not usually removed when a penalty is abated.

Interest accrues on the unpaid tax and on the penalty itself. That means a balance left alone for a year costs more than the penalty percentage suggests, and the difference grows the longer the balance sits. A payment plan reduces the failure-to-pay penalty but does not stop interest on what remains unpaid.

Standing is the cost nobody quotes

For California entities, the third cost can be the largest. An LLC that misses its Statement of Information faces a $250 penalty, and the Secretary of State can suspend the entity. A suspended LLC cannot conduct business, enforce contracts, or use the courts to collect a debt. The tax may be $800; the consequence is that the business loses the legal capacity it was formed to provide.

The Franchise Tax Board can suspend an entity for failing to file or pay, and the two suspensions interact — you may need to resolve one before the other clears. For nonprofits, the IRS can automatically revoke tax-exempt status after three consecutive years of not filing the 990 series.

Standing also matters commercially. Leases, loan covenants, and vendor agreements often require a certificate of good standing. A company can be profitable, current on its bills, and still fail a routine vendor check because a biennial form went to an old address.

Which one bites first

The order of exposure depends on whether money is owed. If there is a balance, the failure-to-file penalty grows fastest, so filing late but filing is usually better than waiting until the return can be paid. If there is no balance, the direct penalty may be modest, but standing and interest on any related liability still matter.

A useful sequence in almost every case: file the return, pay what you can, then ask for relief, then set up a plan for the rest. Filing first stops the largest clock. Paying what you can stops the smaller one from growing on the full balance. Relief and a payment plan are conversations you can have after the return exists.

What relief can and cannot fix

The IRS offers first-time abatement for taxpayers with a clean three-year record: one penalty type can be removed for one tax period. Reasonable cause relief exists for situations with documentation — illness, records destroyed in a disaster, reliance on a professional's incorrect advice. California has its own relief process, and the EDD can adjust penalties for good cause.

Relief generally addresses the penalty, not the interest, and it does not restore standing automatically. If an entity was suspended, the reinstatement process is separate from the tax balance.

The week after a missed date

Do five things in this order. File the return, even if it is late. Pay something, even if it is not the full balance. Read the notice for the exact form and period it references, because one late filing often produces several notices. Ask for penalty relief in writing if the record supports it. Then fix the calendar so the same date does not arrive unannounced next year.

None of this requires panicking. It requires treating the penalty, the interest, and the standing as three separate problems, each with its own clock.
